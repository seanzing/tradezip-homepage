How Quickly Should You Respond to Angi and Thumbtack Leads?

On Angi and Thumbtack, the contractor who responds first wins most of the time. Here's what the response window actually looks like, why it's so short, and what you can do about it when you're running jobs all day.

By Elliot Farmer, Head of Sales ·August 19, 2026 · 8 min read

The most consistent thing I hear from contractors who use Angi or Thumbtack and aren't happy with the results: "I respond, but by the time I call them, they've already hired someone else."

The follow-up question I always ask: "How long after receiving the lead are you calling?" The answer is usually somewhere between two hours and two days.

That's the problem. On these platforms, the response window that matters is measured in minutes — not hours, definitely not days.

The response-time problem most contractors have

Here's the dynamic: a homeowner submits a request on Angi or Thumbtack at 11 AM on a Thursday. That request gets sent to multiple contractors simultaneously. The homeowner gets two or three replies within ten minutes, calls the first one who sounds professional, and books them by noon. Your lead notification arrives at 11 AM too. You're on a job. You see it at 1 PM, call at 1:30 PM, and the homeowner has already booked someone.

You paid for that lead. You lost it not because of your pricing, your work quality, or your reputation — but because of timing.

This isn't a rare scenario. It's the dominant pattern on lead aggregator platforms, and it's the primary reason many contractors write off Angi and Thumbtack as ineffective even when the lead quality is decent.

How fast is fast enough?

The competitive window on most lead platforms is under five minutes. Data from lead platforms consistently shows that response rates drop dramatically after the first five minutes — contractors who respond in that window close a significantly higher proportion of their leads than those who respond later.

Practically speaking, the reality for most contractors is:

  • Under 5 minutes: Best case — you're in a position of advantage over most competitors
  • 5–30 minutes: Still competitive if the homeowner hasn't already committed to someone else
  • 30 minutes–2 hours: Significant reduction in conversion likelihood; some leads are already gone
  • Over 2 hours: Most leads have already been closed by a competitor

The implication is uncomfortable: if you're running jobs during the day and checking leads when you get home at 6 PM, you're paying for leads you have almost no chance of converting.

Why response time matters so much on these platforms

Homeowners on Angi and Thumbtack are usually in a decisional frame when they submit a request. They've already decided they want to hire a contractor. They're comparing options — and they're comparing whoever responds first.

First-response advantage is compounded by how these platforms present contractors to homeowners. The first contractor who responds often appears higher in the homeowner's view. Platforms also algorithmically reward contractors with high response rates by showing their profiles more prominently — making fast response a self-reinforcing advantage.

The homeowner psychology also matters: once they've talked to a contractor who seems competent and gives them a reasonable quote, many homeowners stop comparing. They've found someone. A contractor who calls an hour later is asking them to reopen a decision they've already mentally closed.

What makes fast response hard

The problem isn't that contractors don't want to respond fast. It's that fast response is genuinely hard when you're on a job, on a roof, or under a sink at the moment the lead comes in.

Structural barriers to fast response:

  • Being on another job and unable to stop and make a call
  • Notifications going to an email you check infrequently
  • No one else to respond on your behalf
  • Crafting a response feels like it takes longer than the job you're currently on

The solution isn't "check your phone more" — that's not practical and it introduces safety and quality issues on the job. The solution is systems that handle the first response automatically.

Practical solutions that actually work

Automated first response

The most effective solution: an automated text or message that goes out the moment a lead comes in, before you've even seen it. The message doesn't have to close the job — it just has to establish contact and buy you time.

An effective automated first response: "Hi [Name], thanks for reaching out about your [job type]. I'm on another job right now but I'll call you personally in the next [time]. If it's urgent, you can also reach me at [phone number]."

This does three things: it confirms you received their request, it sets a response expectation, and it positions you as a contractor who's busy because they're in demand — not one who's slow to respond. By the time you actually call, the homeowner already knows who you are and is expecting to hear from you.

An AI receptionist for incoming calls

If the lead notification generates a phone call rather than a message, having an AI receptionist that answers, qualifies the lead, and lets the caller know you'll call back personally handles the same problem on the voice channel.

Response time as a system, not a personal habit

The contractors who convert the highest percentage of platform leads have made response time a system, not a daily intention. They have an automated first-touch, a scheduled follow-up, and a clear call-back protocol. They've also made their Angi/Thumbtack notifications go to a phone where they'll actually be seen — not just an email they check twice a day.

Optimize your hours of operation

Many lead platforms let you set hours when you're available to receive leads. If you can't respond during certain hours anyway, pausing lead delivery during those windows improves your response rate and your platform metrics — which improves your profile visibility in a compounding loop.

Beyond the first response

Fast first response gets you the conversation. What you do in that conversation determines whether you get the job.

The most common mistake after a fast first response: the contractor calls, leaves a voicemail, and waits for a callback. One follow-up attempt is rarely enough. A systematic follow-up sequence — call, text, call again the next morning — captures jobs that a single attempt misses.

The homeowner is making a decision in a noisy environment. They might have already talked to three contractors. A second or third touchpoint from you, spaced appropriately, can be the thing that tips the decision your way — especially if other contractors have done what most do and only called once.

Also: make sure your website is set up to convert for any homeowners who look you up after seeing your name on the platform. A quick Google search before calling you back is standard behavior — your profile, your reviews, and your website all need to close the loop.

Key takeaways

  • The response window that matters on Angi and Thumbtack is under five minutes. Contractors who respond in that window close significantly more leads.
  • The problem isn't lack of effort — it's structural. Being on a job makes real-time response impossible without systems to handle it.
  • An automated first-touch message — sent the moment a lead comes in — establishes contact and buys you time to call personally.
  • One follow-up attempt is rarely enough. A systematic follow-up sequence (call, text, follow-up call) captures jobs that single-touch approaches miss.
  • Fast response improves your platform metrics, which improves your profile visibility — a compounding advantage over time.
